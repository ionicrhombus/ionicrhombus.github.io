AI-Powered Classroom Simulations

A good AI simulation is designed, not just prompted. Start with one learning goal, build five layers, and your finished worksheet becomes the prompt you give the AI.

The framework

Start with what students should be able to do, then build five layers. Each layer answers one design question, and each builds on the one before it.

  1. 0
    Learning goalWhat should students be able to do afterward?
    One observable skill: “Students will [skill] by [talking with whom] in [what situation].”
  2. 1
    WorldWhere are they, and what is at stake?
    Setting, the student’s role, the stakes, and the constraints (time, rules, realism).
  3. 2
    CharacterWho are they talking to?
    Identity, motivation (what they want from this conversation), voice, and boundaries.
  4. 3
    KnowledgeWhat does the character know, and when do they share it?
    Four tiers: shares freely, shares if asked well, guards, doesn’t know. Each fact gets an unlock condition.
  5. 4
    ModalityWill students type or talk?
    Voice for real-time conversational skills, text for careful reasoning and clean transcripts, or both.
  6. 5
    Evaluation and feedbackHow will students know how they did?
    How the character reacts in the moment, a separate AI coach with your rubric, and your own debrief.

Three core principles across the layers

  • Give the character a want, not just a role.AI models default to being helpful and agreeable. Motivation creates the pushback that makes practice real.
  • Knowledge gating is where the learning happens.If the character shares everything on the first question, there is nothing to practice.
  • Separate the actor from the judge.The character stays in role. A different prompt grades the transcript.

Example: The Potluck Outbreak

An interview simulation for an Intro to Epidemiology course. Students play a county health investigator questioning a diner owner after a foodborne illness outbreak.
